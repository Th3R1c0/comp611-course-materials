Section 1: greedy idea and interval scheduling

[GA-1] Greedy idea

Greedy: take what looks best now, never go back. Correct only with the greedy choice property and optimal substructure. Proofs: exchange argument, staying ahead, cut property.Example: coins 1, 3, 4 to pay 6. Greedy gives 4, 1, 1 (3 coins) but 3, 3 (2 coins) is best.

D&C solves several independent subproblems. DP solves every subproblem and stores them. Greedy makes one choice and solves one subproblem.

[GA-2] Interval scheduling: earliest finish time

Sort by finish time. Pick the first. Then pick each activity that starts at or after the last picked finish. Only earliest finish is always optimal (earliest start, shortest, fewest conflicts can fail).Example: A [1,3), F [3,4), B [2,5), C [4,7): pick A, then F (3 is at or after 3), skip B, pick C.

[4, 6) means it starts at 4 and finishes at 6, and another activity may start at exactly 6.

[GA-3] Interval scheduling: running time

O(n log n) because of sorting. Already sorted by finish time: O(n). Trying every subset: O(2ⁿ).

After sorting, each activity is checked once against the last finish time.

Section 2: MSTs and Prim's algorithm

[GA-4] MST facts, cut and cycle properties

A spanning tree connects every vertex with no cycle and has V − 1 edges. MST = smallest total weight. Different weights means a unique MST.Example: 7 vertices means 6 edges in any spanning tree.

Removing a tree edge disconnects it. Adding any edge makes a cycle.

Cut property: the lightest edge crossing between two groups is in every MST. Cycle property: the heaviest edge on a cycle is in no MST.Example: groups {A, B, C} and the rest, crossing edges 2, 5, 7, so the 2 edge is in the MST.

Only count edges with one end in each group as crossing.

[GA-5] Prim's algorithm

Start at one vertex. Repeat: add the lightest edge from a vertex in the tree to a vertex not in the tree. Stop at V − 1 edges.Example: from P, crossing edges P–Q 4, P–R 2, so add P–R. Then the lightest out of {P, R} is R–Q 1.

Keep a table: tree so far, crossing edges, lightest. The start vertex does not change the total weight.

[GA-6] Prim running time

O(E log V) with an adjacency list and binary heap. O(V²) with an adjacency matrix and a simple minimum search.

The heap holds the crossing edges and hands back the lightest in O(log) time.

Section 3: Kruskal's algorithm

[GA-7] Kruskal's algorithm

Sort all edges, lightest first. Accept an edge unless its ends are already connected (that would make a cycle), in which case reject it. Stop at V − 1 edges.Example: Q–R 1 accept, P–R 2 accept, S–T 3 accept, P–Q 4 reject (P and Q already joined through R).

Prim grows one tree. Kruskal merges a forest of groups. Same total weight, and the same edges if the weights are all different.

[GA-8] Kruskal running time

O(E log V) with a disjoint set forest (union-find), because sorting dominates. With a simple label array: O(E log V + V²).

O(E log E) and O(E log V) are the same thing, because E is at most V², so log E is at most 2 log V.
